REAL-DATA STORY 01 · ARCHIVAL, SIX-STUDY TEACHING SUBSET

The sixth row was included. What changed?

A published R archive contains thirteen historical BCG study records. This deliberately selected classroom exercise begins with five and then adds a large contrasting sixth row. The study was not discovered late: the sequence is a teaching reveal. What changes in the calculation, and what remains unknown?

Scene 1 / pause before the reveal

Do not look at the forest plot yet.

1 · The opening claim

Five selected historical study rows appear to favour vaccination. This is not an exhaustive search; you have inherited an unexplained selection.

2 · The contrasting record

The TPT Madras trial (1980) is already in the original archive. It is added last here only to expose the influence of a large row.

3 · Your evidence decision

Predict what happens to the fixed-effect weighted estimate, then check whether the selection is scientifically justified.

Scene 2 / reconstruct it yourself

Six historical rows. No invented patients.

Counts are transcribed from the official metafor BCG example (dat.bcg / dat.colditz1994). Only IDs 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8 of the original 13 studies are used here. This deliberately nonrepresentative selection demonstrates influence, not vaccine effectiveness.

Selected: 6 of six. Untick any row to experiment. These are post-hoc teaching scenarios and must never be misrepresented as prespecified eligibility rules.

Methods, formula and exact R reproduction

Each trial's log risk ratio is log[(tp/(tp+tn))/(cp/(cp+cn))]; sampling variance is 1/tp - 1/(tp+tn) + 1/cp - 1/(cp+cn). The fixed-effect estimate is inverse-variance weighted. The random-effects demonstration uses DerSimonian–Laird τ² and a normal-approximation interval. Small-k uncertainty and model assumptions are not fully addressed here.

library(metafor)
d <- subset(dat.bcg, trial %in% c(1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8))
e <- escalc(measure="RR", ai=tpos, bi=tneg,
            ci=cpos, di=cneg, data=d)
predict(rma(yi, vi, data=e, method="FE"), transf=exp)
predict(rma(yi, vi, data=e, method="DL"), transf=exp)
# Sensitivity exercises:
subset(e, trial != 8)
subset(e, alloc == "random")

Original source: Colditz GA et al., JAMA 1994;271:698–702. Source analysis and complete thirteen-study table. Endpoint: tuberculosis cases (vaccinated versus control). Assignment method and latitude vary. A real synthesis requires a protocol, complete eligibility and searching, risk-of-bias assessments and clinical interpretation.

Scene 3 / non-religious evidence story

What can a learner responsibly report?

Neither “the vaccine works” nor “the vaccine fails” follows from six chosen rows. Explain the deliberate selection, report what adding Madras changes, show model dependence and allocation heterogeneity, and inspect the other seven archival records before attempting a complete synthesis. Reproducible arithmetic does not establish clinical applicability.

Take-home detective prompt: Write two sentences: (1) What is the difference between data provenance and scientific validity? (2) What would you request before trusting a pooled clinical conclusion?

Scene 4 / three evidence revelations

What the source records really reveal

Good evidence stories have a turning point, but the turning point must come from documented data. These three revelations are drawn from the published R dataset example; they are not invented events or conversations.

The seven unshown records

The source contains thirteen BCG trials; this exercise uses six. Unshown is not necessarily unpublished, suppressed or ineligible. The first investigative action is to inspect the complete source.

Challenge: What would a prespecified eligibility rule need to say?

The influential contrasting trial

Five selected rows give a fixed-effect RR close to 0.405; adding the archival TPT Madras row changes it to about 0.659. Inclusion order is instructional, not historical.

Challenge: Can you reproduce both values and explain their difference?

The model cannot repair selection

The six-row example has I² near 96%, and two trials used alternate allocation. Switching models without accounting for design and selection cannot turn this lesson into a valid clinical recommendation.

Challenge: Which limitations would remain after changing the estimator?
