Early studies suggested intravenous magnesium might help people with suspected acute myocardial infarction. A 1992 trial seemed to strengthen that hope. Then came a much larger experiment. Act I · The hopeful evidence (1991–1992)

A promising signal — and an important question

The seven early studies in the documented R example produce a Peto OR of 0.45 (95% CI 0.28–0.71). This reproduces the historical small-trial synthesis; it is not the five-row classroom selection used below.

In 1992, the independently randomised LIMIT-2 trial recruited 2,316 people with suspected acute MI and reported 28-day mortality of 7.8% versus 10.3% (magnesium versus placebo). These are figures from the published trial abstract, not fictional case numbers.

Those findings were encouraging. But could the early evidence reliably predict what a much larger trial would find?

Act II · The turning point (1995)

One trial. More than 58,000 participants.

The ISIS-4 trial randomised 58,050 patients with suspected acute myocardial infarction across its factorial comparisons. For magnesium, the trial reported 2,216 deaths among 29,011 allocated magnesium (7.64%) versus 2,103 among 29,039 controls (7.24%) at five weeks, with no significant mortality reduction. The treatment comparison used an open control; it was not a blinded magnesium-placebo comparison.

Numbers above come from the ISIS-4 trial and match the archival R dataset; they should not be confused with the pooled classroom Peto OR below.

Act III · What does the reversal actually mean?

3. Separate the finding from the explanation

A larger trial can overturn confidence in an earlier pooled claim, without showing precisely why the studies disagree. Different treatment timing and background care, potential publication bias and model choice have all been discussed in the subsequent literature. These are hypotheses to investigate, not explanations proved by this six-row exercise. Review the later evidence synthesis.
